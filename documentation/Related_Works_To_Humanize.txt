Research on road management and transport technology in Nigeria has produced a body of work that can be grouped into three broad categories: (i) administrative and institutional record-management systems, (ii) geospatial and emergency-response systems, and (iii) automated sensing and computer-vision systems. Although these studies demonstrate the potential of digital tools, they remain fragmented, institution-centred, and largely disconnected from public participation. This section synthesises the audited literature across five variables: system architecture, core features, technology stack, problem solved, and unaddressed gaps and concludes by identifying the gap that the proposed Nigeria Road Management System (NRMS) addresses.

A first cluster of studies focuses on digitising internal government records. Sese (2020) designed a client–server distributed database to centralise traffic offence records for the Federal Road Safety Corps (FRSC) across states and local government areas. The system solved a genuine problem of record fragmentation, but it was restricted to internal officers and offered no citizen-facing reporting, photo uploads, or road-condition tracking. Similarly, Dabo and Yunus (2020) demonstrated that GIS tools could map road facilities in Katsina, yet the resulting system remained an internal database inaccessible to ordinary citizens. Akanbi and Abegunde (2020) extended this institutional logic to a mobile system for vehicle plate registration and accident logging for the FRSC, but their solution excluded road-surface defect reporting and construction-progress monitoring.

Taken together, these systems share a common architectural assumption: the citizen is a passive data subject, not an active participant. Data flows inward to the agency, and no feedback loop returns to the public. This limits both accountability and the volume of ground-level road-condition data that can be collected. Farouq et al. (2017) provide empirical support for the consequences of this limitation: their audit of road-defect logging in Kano State found serious weaknesses and citizen frustration with manual, paper-based reporting. Their study, however, remained a survey-based diagnosis and did not produce a functioning web or mobile platform.

A second cluster applies spatial analysis to road safety. Audu et al. (2021) developed a spatial decision-support system using ArcGIS 10.3, Network Analyst, and GPS to map accident hotspots and calculate shortest ambulance routes in Ibadan. The system improved emergency response times and demonstrated the value of geospatial intelligence in road management. Its scope, however, was entirely post-crash: it addressed emergency medical response and excluded defect reporting, preventive maintenance, and contractor tracking. The system therefore solves a downstream symptom rather than the upstream problem of deteriorating road conditions.

A third cluster explores automated enforcement and monitoring. Umar et al. (2021) built an image-processing client–server system using a k-NN algorithm, MATLAB, and roadside cameras to detect vehicles running red lights through licence-plate detection and optical character recognition (OCR). The system achieved automated violation detection, but it required specialised fixed infrastructure and did not handle citizen reporting or road-condition monitoring. Its architecture is capital-intensive and location-bound, making it difficult to scale to the distributed, low-cost monitoring needs of Nigerian road networks.

Across all three clusters, several cross-cutting limitations emerge. First, public involvement is systematically absent. Toriola-Coker et al. (2020) identified the lack of public participation as a key reason public road projects fail, yet none of the audited systems provides a citizen-facing channel for reporting defects or verifying contractor progress. Second, data collection remains inconsistent. Odinfono and Olawole (2024) found that 55% of traffic crash data collection in southwestern Nigeria still relies on non-standard paper forms, producing inconsistencies between police and road-safety records. Third, existing systems are functionally isolated: each addresses a narrow slice of the road-management problem  offence records, hotspot mapping, or violation detection — without integrating citizen crowdsourcing, contractor milestone tracking, and inspection alerts into a single platform.

The reviewed literature reveals a consistent pattern: existing systems are institution-centred, functionally isolated, and closed to public participation.
